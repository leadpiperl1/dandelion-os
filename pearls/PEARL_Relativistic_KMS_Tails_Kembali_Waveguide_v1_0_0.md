1. Sharing the Pearl — Why Moving Probes Outgrow Symmetric Bell Curves
In 1905, Albert Einstein explained Brownian motion by treating water molecules kicking a speck of pollen as a symmetric bell curve (Gaussian distribution): gentle nudges cluster near the middle, hard kicks are exceedingly rare, and forward and backward kicks are equally unlikely once average drag is subtracted (MIT / SciTechDaily Report). Yet when physicists applied Einstein's 1905 Langevin equation to charm and bottom quarks racing near the speed of light through quark-gluon plasma (QGP)—the trillion-degree fluid of the early universe recreated for ~10⁻²³ seconds at RHIC and the LHC—the math broke down: beyond leading logarithmic approximation, drag and Gaussian diffusion violate Einstein's fluctuation-dissipation relation at every nonzero velocity, preventing standard Fokker–Planck models from reaching thermal equilibrium (Physical Review Letters 137, 192302).

Farther out, however, rare hard collisions form asymmetric exponential tails: a large jolt that brakes a fast-moving quark (giving energy to the plasma) is exponentially more probable than an equally large jolt that boosts it faster, tilted by the exact universal thermal factor exp(v · k_L / T) (Physical Review Letters 135, 242301).
